\begin{abstract}
Large language models answer the same graph question with sharply different
accuracy depending only on how the graph is serialized into text---a robustness
gap we confirm at up to $0.47$ on Llama-3.3-70B. A natural remedy is multi-agent
\emph{debate}: a Critic checks a Proposer's reasoning before it is committed.
Debate rests on a \emph{verification asymmetry}---checking a claim should be
cheaper than producing one---and graph reasoning is an unusually clean place to
test it, because an edge claim is objectively present in the serialization or it
is not. Across three tasks, three encodings, and three seeds
($5{,}400$ instances per condition), we find that debate does raise accuracy
($+0.05$) and does narrow the encoding gap. However, decomposing the debate into
its reasoning scaffold and its verify-and-revise loop shows that the entire gain
comes from asking the model to reason: the loop itself contributes $-0.02$. A
compute-matched majority vote over independent reasoned answers beats debate on
every cell at $1.07\times$ its token cost. The Critic genuinely detects errors
($\phi=+0.36$), but because correct answers outnumber wrong ones roughly five to
one, its false alarms cost more accuracy than its detections repair. Encoding
fragility is real and reasoning reduces it---but the multi-agent structure is not
why.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly asked to reason over graph-structured
data---social networks, knowledge graphs, dependency structures---presented as
text. A recurring and uncomfortable finding is that their accuracy is highly
sensitive to \emph{how} the graph is written down: the same graph and the same
question, serialized under a different but equally faithful convention, can move
accuracy by tens of points \citep{fatemi2024talk}. We reproduce this
\emph{encoding fragility} on Llama-3.3-70B at a striking magnitude: a
\texttt{connected\_nodes} query answered correctly $95.8\%$ of the time under one
serialization drops to $48.8\%$ under another, on the same graphs. A model whose
answer depends this much on a cosmetic choice of notation is not reasoning about
the graph so much as about its surface form.

A natural remedy is to make the model check its own work. Multi-agent
\emph{debate}, in which a Proposer produces an answer and a Critic scrutinizes it
before it is committed, has been reported to improve factuality and reasoning
\citep{du2023debate, irving2018debate}. Debate rests on an implicit
\emph{verification asymmetry}: recognizing that a claim is wrong is assumed to be
cheaper than producing the right claim in the first place. Yet the evidence is
mixed---LLMs often cannot reliably self-correct \citep{huang2024selfcorrect}, and
when debate is placed head-to-head against simple aggregation it does not always
win \citep{choi2025debateorvote}. A difficulty in adjudicating these results is
that in most reasoning domains the intermediate steps a Critic must check are not
\emph{objectively} verifiable, so a failure of debate cannot be separated from a
failure of checking.

Graph reasoning removes that obstacle. An atomic claim in a Proposer's
trace---``node $5$ is adjacent to node $13$''---either appears in the serialized
graph or it does not, so a Critic's job is objectively well-posed and its behavior
can be measured exactly. This makes graphs an ideal testbed for the question we
ask: \textbf{does debate reduce encoding fragility, and if it helps at all, is the
multi-agent structure the reason?}

We run three graph tasks (\texttt{edge\_existence}, \texttt{node\_degree},
\texttt{connected\_nodes}) under three encodings (\texttt{adjacency},
\texttt{incident}, \texttt{friendship}) across three independent seeds, comparing a
zero-shot baseline, a Proposer--Critic debate, and a compute-matched majority vote.
Our findings are:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Encoding fragility is large and significant} on all three tasks,
    unanimously worst under \texttt{friendship} and best under \texttt{incident}
    (\S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Debate raises accuracy and narrows the encoding gap}, replicating
    across all three seeds---the result one would report by stopping here.
  \item \textbf{The gain is the reasoning scaffold, not the debate.} Decomposing
    debate into its first-turn chain-of-thought and its verify-and-revise loop,
    the loop contributes $-0.02$: asking the model to reason helps, having a Critic
    argue about the reasoning does not.
  \item \textbf{A compute-matched majority vote beats debate} on every cell at
    $1.07\times$ its token cost. The Critic does carry real signal, but its
    revisions are net negative because of a precision, not a detection, problem.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The upshot is a positive result wrapped around a negative one: encoding
fragility is real and reasoning genuinely reduces it, but the multi-agent debate
structure---the part that is expensive and elaborate---is not what does the work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM reasoning over serialized graphs.}
A body of recent work studies how well LLMs answer questions about graphs given as
text, and consistently finds that the choice of \emph{encoding}---how nodes,
edges, and structure are rendered into a string---is a first-order determinant of
accuracy. \citet{fatemi2024talk} systematically vary graph encodings and show that
model performance swings widely across them, with no single encoding dominant
across tasks; \citet{perozzi2024graphtoken} pursue the complementary direction of
learning structured encodings rather than fixing a textual one. This
\emph{encoding fragility} is the phenomenon our work takes as its starting point:
we treat cross-encoding accuracy spread as a robustness metric and ask whether a
reasoning procedure can shrink it. % ADD: a GraphQA / LLM-graph-reasoning survey or benchmark citation

\paragraph{Multi-agent debate.}
Debate frames inference as an interaction between models that argue toward a
better answer. \citet{irving2018debate} propose debate as a mechanism for
supervising systems whose reasoning is hard to check directly, and
\citet{du2023debate} report that having multiple LLM instances propose and
critique across rounds improves factuality and commonsense and arithmetic
reasoning. Our Proposer--Critic setup is a deliberately minimal, two-role instance
of this family: a single model produces one answer, then critiques and revises it.
Mechanically this is a form of \emph{self-correction}, whose reliability is itself
contested---\citet{huang2024selfcorrect} find that LLMs largely cannot self-correct
reasoning without an external signal. Crucially, that prior work cannot say whether
the failure is one of \emph{checking} or of \emph{acting on} a correct check,
because its intermediate claims are not objectively verifiable. The verification
asymmetry underlying debate predicts that an objectively checkable domain is exactly
where the loop should finally help; our graph setting makes the claims checkable, so
we can run that test and measure the Critic's detection and false-alarm rates
directly rather than only observing an outcome.

\paragraph{Aggregation as the alternative.}
A simpler way to spend extra inference compute is to sample several answers
independently and aggregate them. Self-consistency \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}
marginalizes over multiple chain-of-thought \citep{wei2022cot} samples by majority
vote and reliably improves reasoning accuracy. This makes voting the natural
control for debate: both spend more compute than a single answer, but debate lets
the attempts \emph{interact} while voting keeps them independent.
\citet{choi2025debateorvote} compare the two and find voting competitive with or
better than debate in several settings. We sharpen this comparison in two ways:
we \emph{match compute} between the two arms by measured token cost rather than by
response count, and we run it on a task where fragility gives debate its best
chance---an objectively checkable domain in which the Critic provably discriminates
right from wrong.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.}
We generate undirected Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs with NetworkX
\citep{hagberg2008networkx}, $5$ to $19$ nodes each and edge probability drawn per
graph, giving a wide spread of densities. For each of three seeds
($7$, $11$, $13$) we draw an independent set of $200$ graphs, and every graph is
rendered under all three encodings, so a per-encoding accuracy difference reflects
the encoding alone and all comparisons across encodings are \emph{paired} by graph.

\paragraph{Tasks and encodings.}
Three tasks span three answer types: \texttt{edge\_existence} (is there an edge
between two given nodes? --- boolean), \texttt{node\_degree} (how many neighbors
does a node have? --- integer), and \texttt{connected\_nodes} (which nodes is a
given node adjacent to? --- a set). Each graph is serialized under three encodings
from \citet{fatemi2024talk}: \texttt{adjacency}, an edge list; \texttt{incident},
per-node neighbor lists; and \texttt{friendship}, natural-language statements. The
first two label nodes with integers, \texttt{friendship} with popular names;
Table~\ref{tab:example} shows one graph under all three, together with the three
tasks. The encodings preserve the graph exactly and differ only in surface form.
Crossing $3$ tasks $\times$ $3$ encodings $\times$ $3$ seeds $\times$ $200$ graphs
gives $9$ cells of $n=600$ each, $5{,}400$ instances per condition.

\paragraph{Model and conditions.}
All conditions use \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo} (FP8) served by
Together.ai. We compare three (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). The \emph{baseline} is a
single zero-shot answer-only response. \emph{Debate} is a Proposer--Critic loop:
the Proposer emits a numbered claim trace and a final answer, the Critic reviews it
against the encoding and returns \textsc{agree} or \textsc{revise}, and the Proposer
revises until agreement or a turn budget is reached; the Proposer and Critic are the
\emph{same} model in different roles. Its first turn is a single-turn
chain-of-thought answer under the same decoding, which lets us separate the
reasoning \emph{scaffold} (baseline\,$\rightarrow$\,turn~1) from the
verify-and-revise \emph{loop} (turn~1\,$\rightarrow$\,final) with no extra runs.
\emph{Majority vote} draws the same Proposer prompt $N{=}3$ times independently at
the model's shipped sampling defaults and takes the mode of the parsed answers; it
differs from debate in exactly one thing---whether the attempts see each other.

\paragraph{Matched compute.}
$N{=}3$ was fixed \emph{before} the run by matching debate's measured token spend
(debate buys $2.6$--$2.9$ turn-1-equivalents per instance). The realized cost is
$1{,}664$ tokens per instance for the vote against $1{,}554$ for debate, a ratio of
$1.07\times$, so the vote is if anything the cheaper arm and any advantage it shows
is not bought with extra compute. The sampling temperature is Llama's shipped
default, chosen before seeing any result; realized answer diversity is reported as a
diagnostic, never tuned.

\paragraph{Metrics and statistics.}
The primary metric is accuracy; the secondary is \emph{encoding fragility}, the
cross-encoding accuracy spread (max\,$-$\,min) within a task. Because encodings are
paired by graph, significance uses paired tests: Cochran's $Q$ as an omnibus across
a task's three encodings, and McNemar's test for pairwise gaps and for
condition-vs-condition accuracy differences; cell accuracies carry Wilson intervals.
The Critic diagnostic (does a \textsc{revise} track the answer actually being
wrong?) is unpaired, one observation per verdict, so it takes a $\chi^2$ association
test reported with the signed $\phi$. We report the fragility spread descriptively,
supported by replication across the three independent seeds rather than by a test of
the spread itself. Finally, \texttt{edge\_existence} is a single-pair lookup whose
trace reduces to one atomic claim, so a claim-by-claim critique has nothing to work
on and its baseline sits near ceiling; it behaves as a \emph{control} rather than a
third fragile task, and we report every result both over all $9$ cells and over the
$6$ cells that exclude it, never dropping it silently.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Encoding fragility is large}
\label{sec:frag}
Baseline accuracy swings sharply with the serialization on all three tasks. On
\texttt{connected\_nodes} it runs from $0.958$ under \texttt{incident} to $0.488$
under \texttt{friendship}---a $0.470$ gap on the same graphs; \texttt{node\_degree}
spans $0.237$ and even the near-ceiling \texttt{edge\_existence} control spans
$0.113$. The effect is highly significant under the paired omnibus (Cochran's
$Q=377$, $148$, $81$ for the three tasks; all $p<10^{-17}$), and its shape is
unanimous: \texttt{incident} is the best encoding and \texttt{friendship} the worst
on every task. \texttt{friendship} is the one encoding that names nodes rather than
numbering them, which is consistent with named entities making a spurious relation
easier to assert. This fragility is the phenomenon the rest of the paper asks a
reasoning procedure to repair.

\subsection{Debate helps---if you stop here}
\label{sec:apparent}
Read at the top line, debate looks like a success. It raises mean accuracy from
$0.793$ to $0.843$ over all nine cells ($+0.050$; McNemar $b/c=351/619$,
$p=10^{-17}$) and from $0.726$ to $0.843$ over the six cells excluding the
\texttt{edge\_existence} control ($+0.117$; $p=10^{-54}$), and the gain replicates
in sign across all three independent seeds. It also narrows the encoding gap
(Figure~\ref{fig:fragility}): the \texttt{connected\_nodes} spread falls
$0.470\rightarrow0.253$ and \texttt{node\_degree} $0.237\rightarrow0.107$, with the
narrowing reproducing $3/3$ on both fragile tasks. The gains concentrate exactly where accuracy was
worst---the three largest per-cell gains are the three lowest baseline cells---so
debate works by lifting the floor, which is what narrows the spread. A paper that
reported only this would conclude that debate improves encoding-fragile graph
reasoning.

\subsection{The gain is the reasoning scaffold, not the loop}
\label{sec:decomp}
Debate's first turn is a single-turn chain-of-thought answer under identical
decoding, so the split baseline\,$\rightarrow$\,turn~1\,$\rightarrow$\,final
separates the reasoning \emph{scaffold} from the verify-and-revise \emph{loop}
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). The scaffold accounts for the entire improvement: it is
worth $+0.071$ over nine cells and $+0.120$ over six. The loop then gives back
$-0.021$ and $-0.003$. Excluding the \texttt{edge\_existence} control does not
rescue the loop; it moves it from \emph{harmful} to merely \emph{inert}. The loop
helps in just one cell of nine and hurts in two. Both losses are on the
\texttt{edge\_existence} control, where the Critic drags accuracy from $0.922$ to
$0.803$. That task has a single atomic claim: there is nothing to critique, so the
Critic only talks a correct answer down. The one-line reading is that \emph{asking
the model to reason helps; having a Critic argue about the reasoning does not}.

\subsection{A compute-matched vote beats debate}
\label{sec:vote}
If the loop is not paying for its compute, the natural comparison is against
spending that compute on independent attempts instead. Majority vote scores
$0.875$ over nine cells against debate's $0.843$ ($+0.032$; $p=2\times10^{-16}$)
and $0.853$ against $0.843$ over six ($+0.010$; $p=0.035$), at $1.07\times$ debate's
tokens, and it \emph{loses no cell}---it is at least as accurate as debate in all
nine and significantly better in three. It beats debate in all three seeds. The full
ordering across the arm is baseline $0.793 <$ debate $0.843 <$ turn~1 $0.864 <$ vote
$0.875$, so debate is the \emph{worst} of the three reasoning conditions and is
beaten even by its own single first turn. The vote's advantage is largest on
\texttt{edge\_existence} ($+0.147$ on \texttt{adjacency}), exactly where debate is
destructive: an independent redraw cannot be argued out of a correct answer, whereas
a Critic can. Because debate and the vote differ in only one thing---whether the
extra attempts interact---this isolates \emph{interaction} as the ingredient that is
not paying off.

\subsection{Why the loop fails: precision, not detection}
\label{sec:mechanism}
The loop's failure is not that the Critic cannot tell right from wrong. Cross-tabbing
every verdict against whether the answer it judged was actually correct, a
\textsc{revise} is strongly associated with a wrong answer ($\chi^2$ on $5{,}825$
verdicts, $\phi=+0.358$; a \textsc{revise} moves the probability the answer is wrong
from the base rate $0.181$ to $0.459$), and the discrimination is strongest
($\phi=+0.69$) precisely on \texttt{edge\_existence}, where the check is a single
lookup. The Critic detects $50.2\%$ of wrong answers. The problem is what it costs:
it also raises a false alarm on $13.2\%$ of \emph{correct} answers, and because
correct answers outnumber wrong ones roughly five to one, those false alarms convert
into more damage than the detections repair. Net across the arm, revisions move
$113$ more answers from right to wrong than the reverse, and are net-positive in only
one cell of nine. The loop fails on \textbf{precision}, not detection---the Critic
knows the answer is wrong on \texttt{edge\_existence} and still, by revising too many
correct answers, makes that task worse.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Reasoning repairs fragility; structure does not.}
The encoding gap does shrink under debate, but the decomposition locates the cause:
the single-turn reasoning scaffold produces essentially all of the narrowing, and a
compute-matched vote reproduces it (Table~\ref{tab:main}). What reduces a model's
sensitivity to how a graph is written down is being asked to reason about it, not the
multi-agent apparatus around that reasoning. We report the spread reduction
descriptively---it replicates across all three seeds, but we do not test the spread
itself---so the claim is that reasoning narrows the gap, not that debate confers
encoding-invariance: fragility stays highly significant under every condition.

\paragraph{The asymmetry can hold and debate still lose.}
Debate's premise---that recognizing a wrong claim is cheaper than producing a right
one---was satisfied here: the Critic discriminates right from wrong ($\phi=+0.36$,
and $+0.69$ where the check is a single lookup). Yet the loop still lost accuracy,
for a reason that is general. A verify-and-revise step changes accuracy by its
detection rate times the fraction of answers that are wrong, minus its false-alarm
rate times the fraction that are right. When a model is already mostly correct---as
Llama-3.3-70B is here, roughly five right for every one wrong---this balance tips
negative unless the Critic's precision is very high, however well it detects.
Objective checkability, the ingredient prior self-correction studies lacked, was not
enough: it sharpened detection, which was never the bottleneck, and left precision
untouched. The lesson transfers beyond graphs: self-verification pays off only where
errors are common or the check is near-perfectly precise, and neither holds for a
strong model on a task it mostly solves.

\paragraph{Spend compute on independence, not interaction.}
Because debate and the vote differ only in whether the extra attempts interact, the
comparison is a clean test of the two ways to spend inference compute, and
independence wins: three independent draws cannot argue each other out of a correct
answer, whereas a Critic can. Given a fixed budget and a task the model already
handles reasonably, sampling and voting is the safer use of it than a self-critique
loop. This does not refute debate in general---it may still help where the base error
rate is high, or where an external, reliable verifier replaces the model's own
Critic---but on encoding-fragile graph reasoning, at matched cost, it is dominated.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:future}
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Single model and graph regime.} Our results come from one model
    (Llama-3.3-70B) on small Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. The net-effect account of
    \S\ref{sec:discussion} predicts the loop's sign depends on the base error rate,
    so harder tasks and weaker models are the test of whether the failure
    generalizes.
  \item \textbf{Single prompt.} We use one Proposer/Critic wording, and the Critic's
    precision may depend heavily on it; varying the prompts would separate a property
    of the method from one of the formulation.
  \item \textbf{Debate structure.} We tested one minimal two-role loop; richer
    structures---more agents, independent proposers that then interact, or multiple
    rounds---may recover value over voting.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
LLM graph reasoning is sharply encoding-fragile, and asking the model to reason
reduces that fragility. A Proposer--Critic debate appears to help, but the gain is
entirely the reasoning it elicits: the verify-and-revise loop is inert-to-harmful,
and a compute-matched majority vote beats debate on every cell. The Critic genuinely
detects errors; it loses because, against a mostly-correct model, its false alarms
cost more than its detections repair. On this task, reasoning helps---and debate is
not why.
